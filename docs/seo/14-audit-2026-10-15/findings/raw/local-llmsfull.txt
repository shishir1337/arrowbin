### Is Your Vibe-Coded App Production Ready? A 2026 Checklist

Page: https://arrowbin.com/blog/vibe-coded-app-production-ready

Published October 15, 2026, by Md. Shishir Ahmed. 13 min read.

TL;DR: A vibe-coded app is production ready when strangers can't read or change other users' data, secret keys never reach the browser, payments are verified and processed once, errors alert a person, and a backup has actually been restored. Database access rules and leaked keys are behind the public incidents so far, so check those first. Fix the app in place when its data model is sound; rebuild only the part whose data model, auth or payment flow is wrong at the root.

Key takeaways:
- A vibe-coded app is production ready once someone has checked its data access rules and secret keys, its payment handling, and whether a backup can actually be restored.
- Missing database access rules are the most serious common gap; the public Lovable and Moltbook incidents both came down to Row Level Security.
- The Supabase publishable key is meant to be public, while the secret key must never reach a browser, a shipped app or Git.
- Payment webhooks need a signature check and a record of processed event IDs, because providers resend events.
- Fix in place when the data model is sound; rebuild only the module whose data model, auth or payment flow is wrong at the root.
- Bring in an engineer before you charge money or store sensitive data, and keep AI tools for drafts that go through review.
- Restore a backup at least once before launch. Until you do, you don't know your recovery time.

Questions and answers:

**Is Lovable safe for production?**
It can be, but safety depends on configuration rather than the platform. Every table needs correct Row Level Security, secrets have to stay server-side, and auth settings should allow only your real domain. Lovable's own documentation says its scanners cannot guarantee complete security, so get a human review before you handle payments or personal data.

**Can I keep using AI coding tools after a developer cleans up my app?**
Yes. Keep them for prototyping new screens and small changes. The rule is that every change gets reviewed and tested before it goes out through the deploy pipeline.

**How long does it take to make a vibe-coded app production ready?**
It depends on what the audit finds. Missing guards like RLS policies or webhook checks can take days, while a wrong data model or auth flow can take weeks. Get a written audit before you agree a timeline.

**What is the biggest security risk in AI-built apps?**
Data access rules. Public keys are designed to sit in the browser, so the real risk is a database that trusts them without row-level rules. That is what the reported Lovable and Moltbook exposures had in common.

**Do I need to switch frameworks to fix an AI-built app?**
Usually not. Most fixes happen inside the existing stack, especially if it's React or Next.js on Postgres. Change frameworks only when the current one blocks a hard requirement you can name.

**What should I prepare before hiring someone to fix my app?**
Repository access, a list of the user flows that must keep working, admin access to your hosting and database accounts, and any errors users have reported. With those, an audit can start on day one.

**What is a vibe coding cleanup specialist?**
A developer or team that takes over AI-generated code and makes it fit for production, starting with an audit. The job title is new, but the work is ordinary senior engineering. It means security review and access rules first, then tests and a deploy pipeline.

**Is building an MVP with AI tools a waste of money?**
No. It's a cheap way to test whether anyone wants the product. The cost comes later, if the app handles payments or personal data without anyone reviewing it.

### WordPress to Next.js Migration: Steps, SEO and Cost Drivers

Page: https://arrowbin.com/blog/wordpress-to-nextjs-migration

Published October 15, 2026, by Md. Shishir Ahmed. 15 min read.

TL;DR: A WordPress to Next.js migration pays off when slow pages, plugin upkeep or custom features are costing you money, and it's rarely worth it when editors publish daily on a site that already works. Most of the risk sits in the URLs: map every old address, give each one a permanent 308 redirect to its closest new page or a 410 Gone if you removed it on purpose, and keep those redirects for at least a year. Google says to expect temporary ranking movement during any site move, so settle the redirect map and the plan for plugins and store data before anyone writes code.

Key takeaways:
- Move to Next.js when speed, plugin upkeep or custom features cost you money; stay on WordPress when editors publish daily and the site works.
- The redirect map is the migration: every old URL gets a 308 to its closest new page, or a 410 if it was removed on purpose.
- Next.js returns 308 for permanent redirects, and Google treats 308 the same as 301.

- Budget roughly 15–20% of the build cost per year to own custom software (Arrowbin estimate), which is $9,000–$12,000 a year for a $60,000 SaaS MVP.
- Maintenance is four kinds of work: fixing bugs, adapting to platform changes, improving existing features, and preventive work like backups and patches.
- Platform deadlines make upkeep non-optional: Google Play requires API level 36 for app updates from August 31, 2026, and PostgreSQL 14's final release is on November 12, 2026.

- A retainer suits most live products and pay as you go suits stable internal tools; an in-house hire makes sense once there's full-time product work.
- A good plan spells out monitoring, updates, tested backups, bug fixes, a monthly report and a written response time for critical issues.
- Upgrade one major version at a time; three at once turns routine upkeep into a project.

Questions and answers:

**Is 15–20% a year normal for software maintenance?**
It's our planning estimate for custom software, not an industry statistic. Simple, stable tools can need less, while products with regulated data or many integrations usually need more. Check it each year against what you actually spent.

**Does a maintenance plan include hosting?**
Usually not. A maintenance plan pays for engineering work such as monitoring, updates and fixes, while bills from your host, domain registrar or API providers go to those companies directly. Our 15–20% planning band still counts them, because they're part of owning the software.

**Can I stop maintenance once the software is stable?**
You can pause new features, but you can't pause the platforms underneath. Security patches, runtime end-of-life dates and app store requirements keep arriving on dates you don't control, so a minimum level of upkeep has to continue.

**What happens if my Android app isn't updated for Google Play's requirements?**
From August 31, 2026, Google Play requires new apps and app updates to target Android 16 (API level 36), so you can't ship even a bug fix until the target is raised. Existing apps that target Android 14 (API level 34) or lower are only available on devices running that version or older, which hides them from new users on newer phones.

**Is a maintenance retainer worth it for a small business?**
If your software takes payments, stores customer data or runs daily operations, yes, because someone has to watch it and patch it on a schedule. A very simple internal tool that rarely changes can be fine on pay as you go.

**Can unused retainer hours go toward new features?**
On our plans, yes: hours not needed for upkeep go into improvements from a list we agree with you. Policies differ between providers, so ask how unused hours are handled before you sign.

**Will you maintain software another team built?**
Yes. We start with an audit of the code and infrastructure, fix the urgent risks first, then take over routine maintenance with a plan to move the product forward.

**Do mobile apps cost more to maintain than web apps?**
Usually, if you ship on both iOS and Android. Start from the same 15–20% of build cost a year and lean toward the top of it, because each store sets its own yearly requirements. In 2026 Apple required builds with Xcode 26 from April 28, and Google Play required API level 36 for updates from August 31.

### How to Choose a Software Development Company in 2026

Page: https://arrowbin.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-software-development-company

Published April 28, 2026, updated June 18, 2026, by Md. Shishir Ahmed. 13 min read.

TL;DR: To choose the right software development company, weight proven track record, communication and process far above price. Shortlist three to five firms, review live work, ask who builds your project and who owns the code, and watch for red flags such as suspiciously low quotes and vague timelines.

Key takeaways:
- Weight proven track record, communication, technical depth and process above price.
- Shortlist 3–5 firms and review live products you can actually open and use.
- Ask every firm the same questions, especially who builds it and who owns the code.
- Choose an agency for end-to-end products, freelancers for small tasks, in-house when software is your core.
- Treat too-good-to-be-true quotes, vague timelines and weak communication as red flags.
- Score candidates against consistent criteria and decide on overall fit, not the lowest number.

Questions and answers:

**Should I hire freelancers or an agency?**
Freelancers can be great for small, well-defined tasks. For anything ongoing or complex, an agency gives you a full team across design, engineering, QA and project management, plus continuity if one person is unavailable.

**How important is location and time zone?**
Less than it used to be. What matters is overlap for real-time communication and clear, reliable processes. Arrowbin works with clients worldwide, across time zones.

**What questions should I ask a software development company?**
Ask to see and use their live work, who exactly will build your project and how senior they are, how they communicate progress, what their testing and security process is, whether you own the code, and what post-launch support looks like.

**How do I know if a development company is reliable?**
Reliability shows up as live, usable products, references who'd hire them again, a clear process for testing and communication, and honest, responsive behaviour during the sales conversation. Vagueness and slow replies are the warning signs.

**Should I always choose the cheapest quote?**
No. The cheapest quote often leaves out discovery, design or QA, or relies on junior developers, which leads to rework that costs more overall. Compare what's included and who's building it, then treat price as a tiebreaker.

**Do I own the source code if an agency builds my software?**
You should. Confirm in writing that you own the source code, accounts and infrastructure outright. A trustworthy partner has no problem with this; reluctance here is a serious red flag.

### How Long Does It Take to Build an MVP in 2026?

Page: https://arrowbin.com/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-build-an-mvp

Published April 10, 2026, updated June 16, 2026, by Md. Shishir Ahmed. 12 min read.

TL;DR: Most well-scoped MVPs take 6–14 weeks from kickoff to launch in 2026: a simple tool in 6–8 weeks, a standard MVP in 10–14, and a complex SaaS MVP in 14–20. The timeline tracks scope, clarity, integrations and how fast you make decisions. The fastest path is ruthless prioritization.

Key takeaways:
- Most well-scoped MVPs launch in 6–14 weeks: simple tools in 6–8, complex SaaS in 14–20.
- Scope is the biggest lever. The fewer features in v1, the faster you ship and learn.
- An MVP is a complete, working product for one core workflow, not a rough prototype.
- Modern MVPs overlap phases, running design, build and QA in parallel to stay fast.
- Scope creep, unclear requirements and slow decisions are the top causes of delay.
- Launch faster by locking scope, reusing components and making decisions quickly.

Questions and answers:

**What's the difference between an MVP and a prototype?**
A prototype demonstrates an idea (often clickable mockups), while an MVP is a real, working product that users can actually use and pay for. The MVP is what you launch to learn from the market.

**Should I build an MVP if I have funding for the full product?**
Usually yes. An MVP tests your assumptions with real users before you invest heavily, which sharply cuts the risk of building the wrong thing. It also gets you to market months sooner.

**How long does it take to build an MVP?**
Most well-scoped MVPs take 6–14 weeks from kickoff to launch. A simple single-workflow tool can ship in 6–8 weeks, while a complex multi-tenant SaaS MVP usually takes 14–20 weeks.

**Can an MVP be built in 4 weeks?**
Sometimes, for a very narrow, single-feature tool with a reused design system and no complex integrations. Most genuinely useful MVPs need at least 6–8 weeks to be stable and usable.

**What's the most common reason MVPs take longer than planned?**
Scope creep. Adding 'just one more feature' during the build is the single biggest cause of slipped MVP timelines. Locking scope before development starts is the best protection.

**How many features should an MVP have?**
As few as possible while still delivering your core value end to end. Often that means one primary workflow plus the minimum around it, such as sign-in and basic data. Everything else can wait for v2.

**What happens after the MVP launches?**
You gather real usage data and feedback, then iterate. That usually means fixing the rough edges users hit first, leaning into whatever is clearly working, and adding the features people keep asking for. The MVP is the start of the product, not the end.

**How much does it cost to build an MVP?**
A simple MVP typically costs $5,000 to $30,000, while a standard MVP with accounts, a database and a few integrations usually runs $30,000 to $90,000. The figure tracks scope, technical complexity and how senior your team is. Our custom software cost guide breaks the ranges down in detail.
